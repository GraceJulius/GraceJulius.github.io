01 — About

Technology, with people in mind.

I'm a senior studying Computer Science with a minor in Philosophy at Lincoln University of Pennsylvania, the nation's first degree-granting HBCU.

I'm interested in the ethics and policy of technology: how technology systems succeed or fail the people they're built for, especially marginalized communities and people with less access to technology. I want to understand what goes on behind the scenes of the tools we rely on, include people in the design process, and help make technology fair, private, and accountable.

My research has approached this from several angles: phishing risks on my own campus, how students perceive the ethics of AI, and how well AI models actually perform on the tasks they're claimed to handle. I also build tools with the same goal, like TheBlip, a scam-protection app for students that works in 29 languages.

    NeurIPS 2026 WorkshopAccepted

    Sensing the Past with Smell

    Joseph Firmansyah, Grace Julius, Ana Julia Santo Lopes, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, Paul Pu Liang

    NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Foundation Models for Time Series (FMTS)

    Summary

    Can a machine tell what used to be in a room? We introduce SmellPast, an olfactory benchmark tracking lingering odor decay across 50+ hours of controlled trials — up to 2 hours after source removal — for rosemary, paprika, black pepper, and nutmeg against negative-control baselines, plus ML models that classify removed substances from residual traces. Built at the MIT Media Lab's Multisensory Intelligence group.

  2. 2026
    NeurIPS 2026 WorkshopAccepted

    MMGO-Bench: A Multimodal Graph Optimization Benchmark

    Mina Samizadeh, Grace Julius

    NeurIPS 2026 Workshop on Mathematical Reasoning and AI (MATH-AI)

    Summary

    Can vision-language models solve weighted shortest-path problems straight from a picture of a graph? MMGO-Bench isolates layout style, node-label format, textual edge support, and prompting strategy across 90 graph instances in three difficulty tiers — 43,200 multimodal and 1,080 text-only trials over five models (Gemma 3, Qwen2.5-VL, LLaVA, MiniCPM-V, Claude Haiku). Honest benchmarks are how we hold capability claims accountable.

TheBlip

The small mistake that snowballs if nobody catches it early.

College is often the first time students handle their own money, and scammers know it — Americans reported about $16 billion lost to fraud in 2025, mostly to imposter scams. TheBlip is one app that helps students check a scam text, pause before sending money, see what a credit-card offer really costs, and recover after being scammed — built so a bank could plug it in through a partner API, widget, and signed webhooks.

Its warnings work in 29 languages, are read aloud in 25, and are built for screen readers and keyboards — because the people most likely to be scammed are often the least served by English-only screens. One rule we never broke: the AI reads and explains; our code does the math.

We measured our own scam check

483 synthetic messages, including messages in 8 languages; a held-out set scored once.

Scams caught74% → 100%
False alarms6% → 8%

The first design missed 42% of non-English scams — a message with no keyword hit never reached the AI. We fixed it, and we published our limits: synthetic data, a small test split, no adversarial testing yet.
